# What Professional Services Firms Get Wrong About IT Risk

**In short:** Most professional services firms treat IT as a cost center to minimize rather than a risk surface to manage — and the real mistake isn't lacking budget, it's deferring basic controls until after an incident forces the issue. Firms that get this right don't necessarily spend more; they spend earlier, on a smaller set of controls that address where risk actually concentrates.

## The Most Common IT Risk Gaps in Professional Services Firms

Professional services firms — law practices, accounting firms, consultancies, agencies — occupy an unusual position in the IT risk landscape. They're rarely the direct target of the largest, most sophisticated attacks; those tend to go after firms with obvious high-value data at massive scale. But professional services firms hold something almost as attractive to attackers: client data, financial records, and communications that clients trust the firm to protect, combined with IT practices that often lag well behind the firm's revenue size.

The gap shows up in a few recurring places. Access control is usually the first: former employees retaining system access weeks or months after departure, shared logins for convenience, and no clear process for revoking access when a contractor's engagement ends. Second is email and communication security — professional services firms run enormous volumes of sensitive correspondence through standard email with minimal additional protection, making them a frequent target for business email compromise and invoice-fraud schemes that exploit exactly the kind of trusted, ongoing client relationships these firms depend on. Third is vendor and subcontractor risk: firms that are careful about their own systems often have little visibility into the security practices of the specialists and subcontractors they bring in on client work.

None of these gaps are exotic. They're the same handful of issues that show up in nearly every post-incident review at a firm this size — which is itself the point. The risk isn't some unknown, advanced threat; it's a known, common one that never got prioritized because it didn't feel urgent until it was.

## What "Good Enough" IT Risk Management Actually Costs

The most persistent myth in this space is that meaningful IT risk management requires enterprise-level spend — a dedicated security team, expensive monitoring platforms, and a budget line that a mid-sized professional services firm simply doesn't have.

That's not accurate for the risk profile most of these firms actually carry. The controls that address the gaps above are disproportionately about process and configuration, not expensive tooling: a documented offboarding checklist that revokes access same-day, multi-factor authentication enabled firm-wide (not just for a handful of admin accounts), a written data-handling policy that spells out what happens to client data at each stage of an engagement, and a basic vendor questionnaire process before bringing on a new subcontractor. Most of this is achievable within weeks, not quarters, and doesn't require new headcount.

Where cost genuinely enters the picture is insurance and legal review — cyber liability coverage and having security and data-handling policies checked by counsel before they go external — and even that is a fixed, budgetable cost rather than an open-ended one. The perception that "real" IT risk management means enterprise spend is what keeps firms deferring the cheap, high-leverage fixes indefinitely, waiting for a budget cycle that would fund a much bigger program than the actual risk requires.

The honest framing: most of the highest-leverage fixes are policy and process work that can be documented and implemented before a single new tool is purchased. Tooling matters, but it's rarely the first or most urgent gap.

## A Self-Assessment Checklist

A firm can run through this in under 30 minutes to get an honest read on where it stands. Each item is a yes/no:

- Is multi-factor authentication enabled on every account with access to client data or financial systems — not just admin accounts?
- Is there a documented process that revokes system access on an employee's or contractor's last day, not "sometime after"?
- Does the firm have a written data-handling policy that a client or prospect could actually read, covering what happens to their data at intake, during the engagement, and after it ends?
- Has every active vendor or subcontractor with access to client data been asked, in writing, how they handle that data?
- Is there a named person — not "IT will handle it" — responsible for security and compliance decisions?
- Has the firm's cyber liability insurance coverage been reviewed in the last 12 months against its current risk profile?

A firm answering "no" to two or more of these has a genuine gap, not a hypothetical one — and every item on this list is addressable without an enterprise security budget.

## Why This Belongs in the Trust Conversation, Not Just the IT Conversation

For professional services buyers specifically, IT risk isn't a back-office concern — it's directly tied to whether a client trusts the firm with their business in the first place. This is the segment most likely to ask pointed questions before signing: how is our data handled, who has access, what happens if something goes wrong. A firm that can answer those questions with a documented policy, rather than a reassurance, has already cleared the highest bar in the buying process for this audience.

That's also why IT risk management isn't a topic that belongs exclusively to an internal IT function. It's a trust asset — the kind of thing that shortens a sales cycle when a prospective client asks the hard question and gets a real answer instead of a shrug.
